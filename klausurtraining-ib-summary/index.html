📄 Who owns the past? (für diese Übung geschriebener Text)
    In 2019 a small museum in the north of England began to do
    something unusual. Instead of waiting for requests, it went
    through its own collection and asked a single question about
    every object: how did this get here? The answer was often
 5  uncomfortable. Around four hundred pieces had been taken
    during colonial rule, many of them by soldiers.
    The museum published the list online. Within a year, families
    and communities from six countries had made contact. Some
    asked for their objects back. Others did not. A delegation
10  from Ghana explained that it wanted the objects to stay in
    England, but under a shared agreement: the museum would keep
    them, the community would decide how they are shown and what
    is written on the label.
    Not everyone was pleased. Some curators warned that the list
15  would empty the museum. Others replied that a collection
    nobody can explain is not worth keeping. The director put it
    more bluntly: a museum that cannot say where its objects come
    from is not a museum but a storeroom.
    Three years later the museum has given back twenty-two
20  objects and lent out forty more. Visitor numbers have risen.

Vier Regeln

  1. Hauptpunkte, keine Einzelheiten. Frag bei jedem Satz: Fehlt etwas Wesentliches, wenn ich ihn weglasse?
  2. Präsens. The museum checks … — auch wenn der Text von 2019 erzählt.
  3. Eigene Worte, keine Zitate. In der Zusammenfassung stehen keine Anführungszeichen.
  4. Keine Wertung. Was du davon hältst, kommt in Teilaufgabe 3.

Die häufigste Punkteinbuße: nacherzählen statt auswählen.

Satzanfänge, die tragen: The text deals with … · The author points out that … · According to the text, … · The main argument is that …
